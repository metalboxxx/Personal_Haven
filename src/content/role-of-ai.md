A model will help write much of this code. However, I believe the skill in the modern landscape is the ability to determine clear goals, have a concrete vision and most importantly, enforcing the constraint of the project scope.

LLMs are famously capable of thinking a thousand ideas without breaks, rest or mental fatigue, given an unlimited amount of time. For the same cost, LLM can spew out more ideas than any humans do, notwithstanding the quality. Human thinking is narrow-minded and deeply provoked, but LLMs are broad-based [1][2]. With enough tries, you can actually do _anything_. Sounds good! But it is a trap for any project.

When you can do _anything_, you lose scope, lose the purpose of the project. One might spend so much time on a feature that is essentially useless. One might focus on an element that actually distract users from the main point. So you need to implement _constraints_, which is deeply connected to how clear your vision, your ambition is, the element that humans have. This idea is borrowed from Pixar methodology. Surprisingly, when the burden of technicality is lifted, many of the creative field principles can be applied.

So in my workflow, I mainly scope out what I want, my business requirements, and let them loose. As a junior developer, I don't have experience to go through pros and cons of the tech stack, so let the agents list them out and let me choose. Setting up guard rails is good and which is the thing I do, but there are too many things to say at the beginning. Only when work progresses does real drift occur. This is where having a clear goal shines. I found more value in iterating and micro adjusting the agents while they were generating than huge blob of text at the beginning. Furthermore, it is unusually hard to adjust when there are too many things to change, like not understanding anything to ask a question, so I like to generate code slowly while keeping a grand picture in mind.

There is still value in coding slow, there is value in going through every line of code. Prof. Tao said it here much better than I can [3], making an analogy with math. To which I see coding and math are strikingly similar, it's the satisfaction of going through each line of code or each part of the equation to reach a conclusion. However, a huge temptation looms when your colleague finishes work 10 times faster than you, and just sitting there. It is even more demotivating when a genius, experienced senior engineer I worked with answered "Let me just ask the chat bot" when I raised a rather difficult design question.

For me, I will try learning code that I presume I will look again in the future like python, model training, git,... or things related to security as proprietary models seldom unleash their full cybersecurity capabilities. Otherwise, libraries, frameworks, I pretend I am a project manager that cares about the outcome and the code is the black box that my engineers (here, AI agents) can understand. They can evaluate, choose, code whatever as long as meeting the functional and non functional requirements, and honestly, this defining action right here is actually a skill. I honestly doubt that any engineer, even a lead, has full control over the source code, they will expertly trace back whenever there is a problem: the thing AI agents can already do.
